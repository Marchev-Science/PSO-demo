A SHORT UNIVERSITY LESSON

Particle swarm optimisation: from intuition to experiment.

PSO is a population-based, derivative-free method for continuous optimisation. Use this lab to connect the update equations to the behaviour you see on the surface.

1. The problem2. The algorithm3. Parameters4. Landscapes5. Experiments6. Interpretation

1. What are we trying to solve?

We seek a position x* in a bounded domain that minimises an objective function f(x). Here, a position has two coordinates, x = (x₁, x₂), so the objective can be drawn as a landscape. In applications, a position might instead represent dozens of design choices, model parameters or controller settings.

A local minimum is better than nearby positions. A global minimum is at least as good as every feasible position. In a landscape with many basins, moving downhill from one starting point may lead to a local minimum far from the global solution.

Each particle is a candidate solution, not a physical object. Its “velocity” is a proposed change in its decision variables. The landscape does not exert a force on it: PSO learns by evaluating the objective and remembering good positions.

Read the visualisation

Teal dots are particles; the ochre cross marks the best solution discovered. Magenta diamonds show the gradient-descent comparison. In the contour map, both axes use equal units, so a circular basin stays circular on any screen. Lighter surface colours indicate lower values. The 3D relief uses a fixed projection and a transformed height to reveal detail.

2. Memory, momentum and a shared guide

For particle i, coordinate d and iteration t, this demo applies:

vᵢ,d(t+1) = w vᵢ,d(t)
+ c₁ r₁ [pᵢ,d − xᵢ,d(t)]
+ c₂ r₂ [g_d − xᵢ,d(t)]
xᵢ,d(t+1) = xᵢ,d(t) + vᵢ,d(t+1)
xᵢ, vᵢ
Current position and velocity of particle i.
pᵢ
Personal best: the best position this particle has visited.
g
Global best: the best position discovered by any particle.
r₁, r₂
Independent uniform random numbers in [0, 1), sampled anew for each particle, coordinate and iteration.

The personal term pulls a particle towards its own experience; the social term pulls it towards the swarm’s best discovery. Random coefficients vary the strength of these pulls. This lets particles overshoot, separate and explore rather than simply follow one deterministic path.

One full iteration

  1. Keep a snapshot of the swarm’s current best position.
  2. Update each velocity using that shared guide, then apply the velocity limit.
  3. Move each particle. Clamp any out-of-bounds coordinate to the boundary and reverse that velocity component at half strength.
  4. Evaluate the objective. Update personal and global memories whenever a strictly better value is found.

Initially, positions and small velocities are random, and each particle’s starting position is its personal best. A fixed seed reproduces the run when the parameters and interaction sequence are the same.

3. How the parameters change behaviour

Swarm size
More particles sample more locations per iteration, but cost more objective evaluations. A larger swarm is not automatically more efficient.
Inertia w
Controls persistence of motion. Lower values damp velocity quickly; larger values encourage longer travel. Excessive inertia can cause sustained bouncing or poor convergence.
Personal c₁
Weights each particle’s own best discovery. Increasing it can maintain different search directions, but may also produce oscillation around personal memories.
Social c₂
Weights the swarm’s best discovery. A strong social pull often speeds clustering, but can collapse diversity around a suboptimal basin.
Velocity cap
Limits each coordinate’s step to a fraction of the full domain width. A small cap makes exploration slow; a large cap lets particles cross basins but may skip narrow features.
GD rate
Sets the initial step size for gradient descent only. Backtracking reduces it when a proposed move fails to improve the objective.

The defaults (w = 0.72, personal and social pulls about 1.5) provide a useful starting point, not an optimal setting for every problem. Parameters interact: changing one can change what works well for the others.

Exploration versus exploitation

Exploration means visiting different regions; exploitation means refining a promising region. Watch how spread-out trails become clustered. Clustering is evidence of consensus, but it does not certify that the consensus is correct.

4. Why the landscape matters

Gradient descent uses local slope information. On a smooth convex bowl, that information is highly useful. PSO instead compares objective values at sampled positions and can move across regions without following the local slope.

This can help on multimodal or flat objectives, but “derivative-free” does not mean “always easier.” Narrow valleys, tiny attraction basins, high dimensionality and expensive evaluations can all make PSO difficult.

Surfaces and their teaching purpose
SurfaceWhat to observe
SphereA smooth convex baseline. Both methods should approach (0, 0).
RastriginRegular local minima. Watch particles move between basins; some gradient starts may also reach the global basin.
AckleyA broad rippled region around a narrow central basin. Local search may settle outside the centre.
RosenbrockA distorted, curved valley leading to (1, 1). Both methods can need many iterations to refine a solution.
Warped ripplesIrregularly shaped basins. Compare the diversity of particle paths with local downhill trajectories.
TerracesFlat, discontinuous steps. Numerical gradients are usually zero away from step edges, while PSO can jump between terraces.

The first five surfaces have a known minimum value of 0. Terraces also reach 0, throughout the region 1.1 ≤ x < 1.6 and −0.7 ≤ y < −0.2; there is no single optimal point.

5. Guided experiments

  1. Establish a baseline. Choose Sphere, seed 42 and the default parameters. Step through the first few iterations. Identify a particle moving away from the current best, and explain how inertia and random pulls could cause that motion.
  2. Create early consensus. On Rastrigin, compare social pull 0.5 with 2.5 while keeping other settings fixed. Reset before each run and use the same seed. Which run clusters sooner? Does faster clustering lead to a better final objective?
  3. Remove the slope. Run Terraces with both methods enabled. Why do many magenta diamonds remain stationary? Why can a PSO particle move even when its local numerical gradient is zero?
  4. Test momentum. On Ackley, compare inertia 0.3, 0.72 and 1.1 over 300 iterations. Record final objective values and describe the trails. Separate observations from explanations you have not yet tested.
  5. Make a defensible comparison. Pick two parameter settings and run each with seeds 1–10. Keep the surface and stopping rule fixed. Export CSV files and report median and spread of final best values, plus success rate using a tolerance you specify in advance.

For each experiment, predict the outcome first, change one factor at a time, and explain any result that contradicts your prediction. To restore the initial defaults, reload the page.

6. Read the evidence carefully

The convergence plot shows the best value found so far, so it cannot increase within a run. Individual particles can get worse while exploring. A flat best-value curve means no improvement has been discovered; it does not prove optimality.

Surface colours and relief heights use logarithmic normalisation. The convergence plot uses a separate log1p transform so zero remains visible. These transformations reveal detail but distort vertical distances. The hover readout, metrics and CSV retain the original objective values.

The gradient comparison is multi-start: one gradient search starts at each particle’s initial position, and its reported score is the best across all those searches. It estimates slopes with central finite differences and uses backtracking with boundary projection. This is one local optimisation method, not a representation of every gradient-based algorithm.

One PSO iteration uses one objective evaluation per particle. Gradient estimation and line search require extra evaluations, which are counted in the interface. Equal iteration counts are therefore not equal computational budgets. For rigorous comparisons, align evaluation budgets or measured runtime and report multiple seeds.

7. Beyond this two-dimensional lab

Real problems often require different variable scales, constraints and noisy or costly objective functions. Normalising coordinates prevents one variable’s units from dominating the search. Constraints need an explicit strategy such as projection, repair or penalty functions; this demo implements only box boundaries.

With noisy objectives, a single lucky evaluation can become a misleading memory. Repeated measurements or uncertainty-aware comparisons may be needed. In higher dimensions, more of the domain remains unexplored, so behaviour seen on a 2D plot may not carry over.

Useful extensions include neighbourhood-based PSO, inertia schedules, diversity monitoring, particle restarts and hybrid local refinement. Each introduces design choices that should be evaluated against a simple baseline.

Stopping rules: this demo stops at 3,000 iterations. In practice, specify an evaluation budget, target tolerance, runtime limit or stagnation criterion. A stagnation rule is a practical decision, not proof of a global solution.
